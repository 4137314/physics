#+title: Complex numbers and phasors
#+summary: A sinusoid \(v(t)=V_m\cos(\omega t+\theta)=\mathrm{Re}[\mathbf Ve^{j\omega t}]\) is represented by the phasor \(\mathbf V=V_me^{j\theta}\); differentiation becomes multiplication by \(j\omega\), so linear ODEs in sinusoidal steady state become algebraic equations in complex numbers.

* Complex numbers
\(z=x+jy=|z|e^{j\varphi}\), \(|z|=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\), \(\varphi=\mathrm{atan2}(y,x)\); Euler \(e^{j\varphi}=\cos\varphi+j\sin\varphi\). Products multiply moduli and add arguments; \(1/j=-j\); \(z\bar z=|z|^2\).

* Method
1. Check that the circuit is LTI and driven by sinusoids of one frequency, in steady state.
2. Replace each source by its phasor (choose cos or sin as reference and stay consistent) [cite:@steinmetz1893].
3. Since \(\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\mathrm{Re}[\mathbf Ve^{j\omega t}]=\mathrm{Re}[j\omega\mathbf Ve^{j\omega t}]\), each derivative is \(j\omega\).
4. Solve algebraically; return to time domain with \(v(t)=|\mathbf V|\cos(\omega t+\arg\mathbf V)\).
Linearity of Re and of the circuit justifies it; it fails for nonlinear elements or different frequencies at once (use superposition per frequency).

* RMS values
\(V_{rms}=\sqrt{\frac1T\int_0^Tv^2\dd t}=V_m/\sqrt2\) for a sinusoid. Mains 230 V rms → 325 V peak.

* Worked example
\(RC\dot v+v=v_s\), \(v_s=10\cos(1000t)\) V, \(R=1\) kΩ, \(C=1\) µF: \((1+j\omega RC)\mathbf V=10\Rightarrow\mathbf V=\frac{10}{1+j}=7.07e^{-j45^\circ}\). So \(v(t)=7.07\cos(1000t-45^\circ)\) V: at the corner frequency, −3 dB and −45°.

* Engineering applications
AC power systems, RF and audio circuit design, the \(e^{j\omega t}\) convention of [[id:maxwell-equations][time-harmonic fields]], I/Q signals in communications (a complex baseband is a phasor that slowly varies).

* Pitfall
Phasors with peak amplitude vs rms amplitude: power formulas differ by a factor 2 (\(P=\tfrac12\mathrm{Re}(\mathbf V\mathbf I^*)\) with peak, \(\mathrm{Re}(\mathbf V\mathbf I^*)\) with rms). State which one you use.
