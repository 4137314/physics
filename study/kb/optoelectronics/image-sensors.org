#+title: Image sensors: CCD and CMOS
#+summary: An image sensor integrates photo-generated charge in each pixel during the exposure (\(N_e=\eta\frac{P\,t_{exp}}{h\nu}\)); a CCD moves the charge packets pixel to pixel to one output amplifier with clocked MOS gates, a CMOS active-pixel sensor converts charge to voltage inside each pixel and reads rows through switches; performance is set by quantum efficiency, full-well capacity \(N_{sat}\), read noise \(\sigma_r\) (dynamic range \(20\log\frac{N_{sat}}{\sigma_r}\)) and photon shot noise (\(\mathrm{SNR}=\frac{N_e}{\sqrt{N_e+\sigma_r^2}}\)).

* CCD: charge transfer
Each pixel is a MOS capacitor biased into deep depletion: a potential well that collects electrons. In a three-phase CCD every pixel has three gates; raising the voltage of the next gate while lowering the current one moves the well — and its charge packet — one gate along. Rows shift into a serial register that shifts to a single charge amplifier. Charge transfer efficiency must be > 0.99999 per transfer (a 4000-column CCD makes ~12 000 transfers). Advantages: uniformity, very low noise, 100% fill factor; drawbacks: high clock voltages and power, slow serial readout, blooming and smear.

* CMOS active-pixel sensor
Each pixel holds a pinned photodiode and 3–4 transistors: reset, transfer gate (4T), source follower (in-pixel buffer) and row select; columns share amplifiers and ADCs. Correlated double sampling (read reset level, then signal, subtract) cancels kTC and offset noise: read noise ≈ 1–2 e⁻. Advantages: low power, on-chip logic and ADCs, random access, speed; drawbacks historically fill factor (transistors take area — solved by microlenses and back-side illumination) and rolling-shutter distortion of moving objects (global-shutter pixels store charge). CMOS now dominates phones, cars and most scientific cameras; CCDs survive in niche astronomy and spectroscopy.

* Figures of merit
QE (Si: up to 90% with BSI and AR coating, falls in the blue and beyond 900 nm, [[id:semiconductor-optical-properties][absorption]]); full-well capacity \(N_{sat}\) (≈ 5000 e⁻ for 1 µm phone pixels, \(10^5\) for 10 µm pixels); read noise; dark current (doubles every ≈ 7 K: cooled sensors for astronomy); dynamic range \(20\log(N_{sat}/\sigma_r)\); colour by a Bayer mosaic (RGGB) and demosaicing. At high light the SNR is shot-noise limited, \(\sqrt{N_e}\), maximum \(\sqrt{N_{sat}}\) — larger pixels give cleaner images.

* Worked examples
1. *Signal*: 3 µm pixel (\(9\times10^{-12}\) m²), irradiance 10 mW/m² at 550 nm: \(9\times10^{-14}\) W → \(2.5\times10^5\) photons/s; QE 0.6, 10 ms exposure: \(N_e=1.5\times10^3\) e⁻; SNR \(=\sqrt{1500}=39\) (31.7 dB); a 3 e⁻ read noise changes it to 38.6.
2. *Dynamic range*: \(N_{sat}=10^4\), \(\sigma_r=3\) e⁻: DR \(=20\log3333=70.5\) dB; best SNR \(=\sqrt{10^4}=100\) (40 dB).
3. *Transfer efficiency*: CTE 0.99999 over 12 000 transfers keeps \(0.99999^{12000}=89\%\) of the charge of the farthest pixel; 0.9999 would keep only 30%.

* Engineering applications
Phone and automotive cameras (CMOS, HDR by multiple exposures), machine vision, astronomy (large cooled CCD and CMOS mosaics), X-ray flat-panel detectors (active-matrix TFT readout of photodiodes under a scintillator), time-of-flight depth sensors, SPAD arrays for LiDAR.

* Exam-style exercise
A CMOS sensor has 1.4 µm pixels, QE 0.7 at 530 nm, full well 6000 e⁻, read noise 2 e⁻, dark current 5 e⁻/s. (a) Dynamic range. (b) Exposure that fills the well at 0.1 W/m². (c) SNR at 1% of full well, including read and dark noise for a 30 ms exposure.
** Solution
(a) \(20\log(6000/2)=69.5\) dB. (b) Pixel power \(0.1\cdot1.96\times10^{-12}=1.96\times10^{-13}\) W → \(5.2\times10^5\) photons/s → \(3.7\times10^5\) e⁻/s: full in 16 ms. (c) \(N_e=60\), dark 0.15 e⁻: \(\mathrm{SNR}=\frac{60}{\sqrt{60+0.15+4}}=7.5\) (17.5 dB); shot noise of the signal still dominates.

* Pitfall
Pixel SNR is not the "dynamic range": DR compares the largest and smallest signals in one exposure, SNR the signal to its own noise; at full well the SNR (≈ 40 dB) is far below the DR (≈ 70 dB).
