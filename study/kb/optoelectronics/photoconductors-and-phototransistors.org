#+title: Photoconductors, phototransistors and Schottky photodiodes
#+summary: Light raises the conductivity of a semiconductor slab by \(\Delta\sigma=e\Delta n(\mu_e+\mu_h)\) with \(\Delta n=\frac{\eta I\lambda\tau}{hcD}\); because a carrier leaving one electrode is replaced at the other until it recombines, the photoconductive gain is \(G=\frac{\tau}{\tau_t}\) with \(\tau_t=\frac{L^2}{\mu V}\) — high gain, low speed (gain × bandwidth ≈ const); a phototransistor amplifies its base photocurrent by \(\beta\); a Schottky photodiode avoids slow minority-carrier diffusion and detects below the gap by internal photoemission.

* Photoconductor (derivation of the gain)
A slab of thickness \(D\), length \(L\) between electrodes, absorbs intensity \(I\): generation rate per volume \(\frac{\eta I}{h\nu D}\), steady excess density \(\Delta n=\frac{\eta I\lambda\tau}{hcD}\) (\(\tau\) = carrier lifetime). With bias \(V\) an electron crosses in \(\tau_t=L/v=\frac{L^2}{\mu_eV}\). Charge neutrality: when it leaves at the anode another enters at the cathode, so each absorbed photon lets \(\tau/\tau_t\) electrons through the circuit before recombination: \(I_{ph}=e\,\frac{\eta P}{h\nu}\,\frac{\tau}{\tau_t}\). The response time is \(\tau\) itself, so \(G\cdot B\approx\frac{1}{2\pi\tau_t}\) is fixed by geometry and bias.

* Phototransistor
A bipolar transistor with a large, exposed base–collector junction: the photocurrent \(I_{ph}\) acts as base current and the collector current is \((\beta+1)I_{ph}\). Gain 100–1000 at the cost of speed (large junction capacitance multiplied by the Miller effect: tens of kHz to a few MHz). Used in optocouplers, slotted interrupters and reflective sensors.

* Schottky photodiode
A thin semi-transparent metal on a semiconductor forms a barrier \(\Phi_B\) with a depletion region at the surface. Photons absorbed near the surface give carriers that are swept by drift — no slow diffusion tail — so Schottky and metal–semiconductor–metal (MSM) detectors reach > 100 GHz; good UV response (absorption near the surface, e.g. GaN, SiC). Photons with \(\Phi_B<h\nu<E_g\) excite electrons in the metal over the barrier (internal photoemission): PtSi/Si arrays detect 3–5 µm infrared.

* Worked examples
1. *Gain*: Si photoconductor \(L=100\) µm, \(\mu_e=1350\) cm²/(V s), \(V=10\) V: \(\tau_t=\frac{(10^{-2})^2}{1350\cdot10}=7.4\) ns; with \(\tau=1\) µs, \(G=135\), bandwidth ≈ \(\frac{1}{2\pi\tau}=160\) kHz.
2. *CdS light-dependent resistor*: 1 µm film, \(\mu_e=0.03\) m²/(V s), \(\tau=1\) ms, 10 W/m² of 550 nm light (bright room): \(\Delta n=\frac{10\cdot0.55\times10^{-6}\cdot10^{-3}}{hc\cdot10^{-6}}=2.8\times10^{22}\) m\(^{-3}\), \(\Delta\sigma=133\) S/m. Electrodes 2 mm apart, 5 mm wide: \(R=\frac{L}{\Delta\sigma Wd}=3\) kΩ (MΩ in the dark); \(\tau_t=13\) µs at 10 V, \(G=75\). Typical LDRs follow \(R\propto E^{-\gamma}\), \(\gamma\approx0.7\), and need tens of ms to respond.
3. *Phototransistor*: \(I_{ph}=1\) µA, \(\beta=100\): \(I_C=101\) µA, into 10 kΩ: 1 V swing without an amplifier.

* Engineering applications
Light sensors in street lamps and cameras (CdS, being phased out for RoHS), IR detectors (PbS, PbSe, HgCdTe photoconductors), optocouplers and encoders (phototransistors), UV flame and solar-blind detectors (Schottky GaN, SiC), high-speed MSM detectors.

* Exam-style exercise
An InSb photoconductor (\(\mu_e=7\) m²/(V s), \(\tau=10^{-7}\) s), electrodes 1 mm apart, biased at 1 V, receives 1 µW at 4 µm with \(\eta=0.6\). (a) Transit time and gain. (b) Photocurrent. (c) Bandwidth.
** Solution
(a) \(\tau_t=\frac{(10^{-3})^2}{7\cdot1}=1.43\times10^{-7}\) s; \(G=0.7\) (no gain: InSb lifetime is short). (b) \(I=e\eta\frac{P\lambda}{hc}G=1.6\times10^{-19}\cdot0.6\cdot\frac{10^{-6}\cdot4\times10^{-6}}{1.99\times10^{-25}}\cdot0.7=1.35\) µA. (c) \(\approx\frac{1}{2\pi\tau}=1.6\) MHz. Raising \(V\) increases \(G\) until heating or sweep-out limits it.

* Pitfall
Gain larger than 1 in a photoconductor does not mean more photons are absorbed: it is the same carrier circulating; noise (generation–recombination) is amplified too, and speed drops in proportion.
