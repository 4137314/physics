#+title: Polarisation, birefringence and wave plates
#+summary: Light polarised at \(\theta\) to a polariser transmits \(I=I_0\cos^2\theta\) (Malus); in a uniaxial crystal the ordinary and extraordinary waves see \(n_o\) and \(n_e(\theta)\), \(\frac{1}{n_e(\theta)^2}=\frac{\cos^2\theta}{n_o^2}+\frac{\sin^2\theta}{n_e^2}\), and acquire a relative phase \(\phi=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(n_e-n_o)L\): a quarter-wave plate (\(\phi=\pi/2\)) turns linear light at 45° into circular, a half-wave plate rotates linear light by \(2\alpha\).

* Polarisation states
Write the transverse field as \(\mathbf E=\left(E_{x0}\hat x+E_{y0}e^{j\delta}\hat y\right)e^{j(\omega t-kz)}\) (Jones vector \((E_{x0},E_{y0}e^{j\delta})\)) [cite:@jones1941]. \(\delta=0,\pi\): linear; \(E_{x0}=E_{y0}\), \(\delta=\pm\pi/2\): circular; otherwise elliptical. Unpolarised light is a random mixture: an ideal polariser passes half of it.

* Malus's law and polarisers
A polariser projects the field onto its axis: \(E\cos\theta\), so \(I=I_0\cos^2\theta\). Polarisers: dichroic sheets (Polaroid, absorb one component), Brewster stacks (reflect s, transmit p), birefringent prisms (Glan–Thompson, Wollaston), wire grids.

* Birefringence
In an anisotropic crystal \(\epsilon\) is a tensor; in a uniaxial crystal (calcite, quartz, LiNbO\(_3\)) one axis (optic axis) differs. A wave with \(\mathbf E\perp\) optic axis sees \(n_o\) (ordinary); the orthogonal one sees \(n_e(\theta)\), with \(\theta\) the angle between \(\mathbf k\) and the optic axis (formula in the summary). Positive crystal \(n_e>n_o\) (quartz 1.553/1.544), negative \(n_e<n_o\) (calcite 1.486/1.658). Off-axis a single ray splits into two (double refraction).

* Wave plates (derivation)
Decompose the input on the fast and slow axes. After thickness \(L\) the slow component lags by \(\phi=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}|n_e-n_o|L\). Quarter wave: \(\phi=\pi/2\); equal components (input at 45°) become \((1,e^{-j\pi/2})\): circular. Half wave: \(\phi=\pi\) flips the sign of the slow component: linear light at \(\alpha\) to the fast axis exits at \(-\alpha\), a rotation by \(2\alpha\). A zero-order plate has the smallest \(L\); multi-order plates (\(\phi=2\pi m+\pi/2\)) are thicker but very sensitive to \(\lambda\) and temperature.

* Optical activity, Faraday effect and isolators
Optically active media (quartz along its axis, sugar solutions) rotate linear polarisation by \(\rho L\), the same sense for both directions. The Faraday effect rotates by \(\theta=\mathcal VBL\) (Verdet constant \(\mathcal V\)) and is *non-reciprocal*: on the way back the rotation adds. A 45° Faraday rotator between two polarisers at 45° to each other passes light forward and blocks the reflection (rotated to 90°): the optical isolator that protects lasers from feedback.

* Worked examples
1. *Three polarisers*: unpolarised \(I_0\) through polarisers at 0°, 45°, 90°: \(I=\frac{I_0}{2}\cos^245^\circ\cos^245^\circ=I_0/8\); without the middle one, zero.
2. *Quartz quarter-wave plate* at 633 nm (\(\Delta n=0.009\)): \(L=\frac{\lambda}{4\Delta n}=17.6\) µm (half-wave: 35.2 µm). Calcite (\(\Delta n=0.172\)) would need 0.92 µm: too thin to handle, so calcite is used for polarisers, quartz and mica for plates.
3. *Calcite at \(\theta=30^\circ\)* from the optic axis: \(n_e(30^\circ)=\left(\frac{0.75}{1.658^2}+\frac{0.25}{1.486^2}\right)^{-1/2}=1.609\).
4. *Faraday rotator* in TGG (\(\mathcal V\approx-134\) rad T\(^{-1}\)m\(^{-1}\) at 633 nm) with 1 T: 45° needs \(L=\frac{\pi/4}{134}=5.9\) mm.

* Engineering applications
LCD screens (twisted nematic cell between crossed polarisers rotates the light by 90° when off), 3D cinema (circular polarisation), optical isolators and circulators, polarisation-maintaining fibres (stress birefringence), [[id:electro-optic-modulators][Pockels cells]] (voltage-controlled wave plates), ellipsometry, stress analysis by photoelasticity, polarisation-mode dispersion in [[id:fiber-dispersion-and-attenuation][fibres]].

* Exam-style exercise
Linearly polarised light at 633 nm, at 30° to the fast axis, crosses a quartz plate 35.2 µm thick. (a) What is the plate? (b) Output polarisation. (c) An analyser at 90° to the input: transmitted fraction?
** Solution
(a) \(\phi=\frac{2\pi}{0.633}\cdot0.009\cdot35.2=\pi\): half-wave plate. (b) Linear, at \(-30^\circ\) to the fast axis, i.e. rotated by 60°. (c) Angle between output and analyser \(90^\circ-60^\circ=30^\circ\): \(I/I_0=\cos^230^\circ=0.75\).

* Pitfall
A half-wave plate rotates by *twice* the angle between the input and its axis, not by 90°; and a quarter-wave plate gives circular light only if the input is at exactly 45°, otherwise elliptical.
