#+title: Single-mode laser diodes: DFB, DBR, VCSEL, quantum wells and efficiencies
#+summary: A Fabry–Perot chip lases on several longitudinal modes; a grating in the cavity (DFB) or at its ends (DBR) reflects only near \(\lambda_B=\frac{2\bar n\Lambda}{q}\), and a uniform DFB lases on the two modes \(\lambda_m=\lambda_B\pm\frac{\lambda_B^2}{2\bar nL}(m+1)\) (a quarter-wave shift picks one); a VCSEL's µm cavity has mode spacing larger than the gain width; quantum wells shift the emission by the confinement energies \(E_n=\frac{n^2h^2}{8m^*d^2}\); efficiencies: \(\eta_{EQE}=\frac{P_o/h\nu}{I/e}\), \(\eta_{EDQE}=\frac{e}{E_g}\frac{\dd P_o}{\dd I}\), \(\eta_{PCE}=\frac{P_o}{IV}\).

* Lateral confinement
Gain-guided stripe lasers inject current through a narrow contact; index-guided buried heterostructures surround the active stripe with lower-index material on all sides: lower threshold, stable single *transverse* mode, elliptical output beam (larger divergence across the thin junction). Longitudinal mode selection is the job of the gratings below.

* DFB and DBR (derivation of the DFB modes)
A corrugation of period \(\Lambda\) couples forward and backward waves when \(2\bar n\Lambda=q\lambda\) [cite:@kogelnik1972]. Coupled-mode theory ([[id:bragg-gratings][Bragg gratings]]) gives a stop band around \(\lambda_B\) where no mode propagates; the allowed modes sit symmetrically outside it, spaced like those of a cavity of length \(L\): \(\lambda_m=\lambda_B\pm\frac{\lambda_B^2}{2\bar nL}(m+1)\), \(m=0,1,\dots\) The two \(m=0\) modes have equal threshold; a \(\lambda/4\) phase shift in the middle of the grating (or an asymmetric facet coating) places one mode at \(\lambda_B\) with the lowest threshold: single-frequency operation with side-mode suppression > 40 dB. In a DBR laser the gratings are passive end mirrors; separate electrodes on the grating and phase sections tune the wavelength.

* VCSEL and external-cavity lasers
A VCSEL has a ≈ 1λ cavity with quantum wells between two DBR mirrors of 20–30 GaAs/AlAs pairs (\(R>99.5\%\), [[id:thin-film-optics-and-coatings][dielectric mirrors]]): the mode spacing (tens of nm) exceeds the gain width, so one longitudinal mode lases; circular, low-divergence beam; threshold below 1 mA; wafer-level testing; arrays [cite:@soda1979;@dingle1974]. External-cavity lasers close the cavity with a grating outside an AR-coated chip: kHz linewidths, tens of nm of tuning.

* Quantum-well lasers
A well of thickness \(d\) quantises the motion across it: \(E_n=\frac{n^2h^2}{8m^*d^2}\) above the band edge for electrons and holes ([[id:heterojunctions-and-quantum-wells][quantum wells]]). The step-like density of states puts more carriers at the band edge: lower transparency density, lower \(J_{th}\), higher differential gain, less temperature sensitivity; the emission shifts to \(E_g+E_{1e}+E_{1h}\). Strained wells and multiple wells (MQW) refine this.

* Efficiencies and temperature
- \(\eta_{EQE}=\frac{P_o/h\nu}{I/e}\) (overall photons per electron); \(\eta_{EDQE}=\frac{e}{E_g}\frac{\dd P_o}{\dd I}\) (slope above threshold); \(\eta_{PCE}=\frac{P_o}{IV}\).
- \(\lambda\) of a Fabry–Perot laser follows the gain peak (≈ 0.3–0.5 nm/K), jumping between modes (*mode hops*); a DFB follows \(\bar n(T)\) continuously (≈ 0.1 nm/K): telecom lasers sit on thermo-electric coolers.

* Direct modulation and chirp
Modulating the current modulates output power up to the relaxation-oscillation frequency (10–30 GHz, [[id:semiconductor-laser-diodes][laser diodes]]); the carrier density also modulates the index, so the frequency follows the current (chirp, Henry factor). Chirp broadens the spectrum and, with [[id:fiber-dispersion-and-attenuation][fibre dispersion]], limits directly modulated links at 1550 nm to tens of km at 10 Gb/s; longer links use a CW DFB and an external modulator.

* Worked examples
1. *DFB wavelength*: \(\bar n=3.5\), \(\Lambda=0.22\) µm, first order: \(\lambda_B=2\cdot3.5\cdot0.22=1.540\) µm. For \(L=400\) µm the two lowest modes are at \(\lambda_B\pm\frac{1.54^2}{2\cdot3.5\cdot400}\) µm \(=\lambda_B\pm0.85\) nm (1539.15 and 1540.85 nm).
2. *GaAs well*, \(d=10\) nm, \(m_e^*=0.067m_0\), \(m_{hh}^*=0.50m_0\): \(E_{1e}=56\) meV, \(E_{1h}=7.5\) meV; emission at \(1.424+0.064=1.488\) eV → 833 nm instead of 871 nm; \(E_{2e}=4E_{1e}=225\) meV.
3. *Efficiencies* of a 1310 nm DFB: \(I_{th}=10\) mA, 8 mW at 40 mA and 1.5 V: slope 0.267 W/A, \(\eta_{EDQE}=\frac{0.267}{0.946}=28\%\), \(\eta_{EQE}=\frac{8}{0.946\cdot40}=21\%\), \(\eta_{PCE}=\frac{8}{60}=13\%\).
4. *Temperature*: over +20 K an FP laser's gain peak moves ≈ 10 nm (several mode hops); a DFB moves ≈ 2 nm, more than a 100 GHz (0.8 nm) DWDM channel: thermal control is mandatory.

* Engineering applications
DFB lasers for DWDM transmitters (1550 nm ITU grid), DBR and sampled-grating tunable lasers, VCSEL arrays at 850 nm for datacom and 940 nm for face recognition and LiDAR, external-cavity lasers for coherent communications and spectroscopy, QW and quantum-dot lasers for silicon photonics.

* Exam-style exercise
A DFB laser must emit at 1550 nm with \(\bar n=3.3\). (a) First-order grating period. (b) Second-order period (easier lithography). (c) With \(L=300\) µm, spacing of the two \(m=0\) modes of a uniform grating. (d) Why is a \(\lambda/4\) shift used?
** Solution
(a) \(\Lambda=\frac{\lambda}{2\bar n}=235\) nm. (b) \(q=2\): 470 nm. (c) Each is \(\frac{1.55^2}{2\cdot3.3\cdot300}\) µm \(=1.21\) nm from \(\lambda_B\): 2.43 nm apart. (d) The two modes have equal threshold, so the laser may hop between them; the \(\lambda/4\) phase shift creates a single mode at \(\lambda_B\) inside the stop band with the lowest threshold.

* Pitfall
The DFB lases *beside* the Bragg wavelength unless the grating is phase-shifted: answering "\(\lambda=\lambda_B\)" for a uniform DFB misses the stop band.
